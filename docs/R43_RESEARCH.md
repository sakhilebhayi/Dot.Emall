# Dot.Emall research brief: how marketplaces really work and where people are frustrated

Date: 2026-11-08. Scope: research only, no repo touched.

How to read the evidence tags. [S#] means a source in the list at the end that I fetched or saw in search results this session. (K) means well-known public behaviour I am stating from general knowledge and did NOT re-verify in this run, so treat it as a hypothesis to check before it drives a decision. "Vendor" means the source sells something related to the claim. "Anecdote" means one person or one forum thread. Where I found nothing, I say GAP.

Two limits up front. First, several primary pages blocked fetching (Takealot help centre, Paystack main docs, lawlibrary.org.za), so Takealot policy details come from secondary pages. Second, per-platform UX descriptions (product page layout, wishlist, loyalty) are mostly (K); the hard evidence in this brief is on fees, regulation, checkout research, Competition Commission findings, complaint patterns and South African payment and delivery data.

## 2. The ten most repeated customer frustrations

1. **Fake or incentivised reviews.** Which? documented review brokers and suspicious five-star bursts on Amazon [S41]. Regulators have moved: the FTC rule (effective 21 Oct 2024) bans fake and paid reviews and review suppression [S17]; the UK DMCC Act banned fake reviews from 6 April 2025 and the CMA opened fake-review investigations in 2026 [S22]; the EU requires traders to say whether and how they check that reviewers bought the product [S23, vendor-written, verify against the directive]. SA: no equivalent rule found in this run (GAP), but CPA misrepresentation law applies in principle (K).
2. **Dark patterns and false urgency or scarcity.** Amazon's USD 2.5bn Prime settlement is the clearest hard evidence [S18]. BEUC's Temu complaint alleges manipulative design and difficulty closing accounts [S19]. Evidence specifically on countdown timers and "only 2 left" claims is thinner: BEUC names dark patterns generally, and I did not find a measured study in this run (GAP). Design rule: no timers or scarcity text unless backed by real stock or a real end time.
3. **Fake discounts and reference prices.** Which? tracking: in 2019, 85 percent of Black Friday "deals" were the same price or cheaper in the prior six months; in 2022, 98 percent were the same or cheaper at other times in the year; a later report said 2 percent were cheapest on the day [S20 (several Which? reports, retold by news)]. The EU Omnibus rule makes "was" the lowest price of the previous 30 days [S21]. SA: the NCC runs false-pricing campaigns under CPA s23 [S11]; I found no explicit SA 30-day rule (GAP).
4. **Delivery and other costs revealed late.** Baymard: 40 percent of US shoppers who abandoned cited extra costs (shipping, tax, fees) and 12 percent could not see total cost up front [S1]. The UK bans drip pricing from April 2025 [S22]. SA: townships often add R100 to R150 courier surcharges per Pargo's CEO (vendor, July 2024) [S25].
5. **Search relevance and sponsored results.** Baymard's 2026 benchmark found 56 percent of sites have inadequate search, only 19 percent of autocomplete reaches best performance, and 69 percent mishandle misspellings (as relayed by search summaries, full data paywalled) [S4]. The Competition Commission found Takealot's Buy Box and highlighted-product display disadvantaged marketplace offers and ordered change [S12].
6. **Counterfeits.** Allegations that Takealot's Buy Box rewards "parallel import" replica sellers (Carte Blanche, July 2024; reported not adjudicated) [S28]. The CGSO says most counterfeit complaints it sees involve clothing, footwear and cosmetics, and it cannot act on overseas sellers [S28]. Online purchases were 19 percent of 12,207 CGSO complaints in 2024/25 [S27].
7. **Slow or opaque refunds.** Hellopeter's AI scoring puts "Refunds, Returns and Consumer Rights" at 0.9 for Takealot, cites weeks of chasing, and a store-credit refund after a pre-shipment cancel (self-selected reviews; the analysis is machine-generated) [S13]. Takealot's own help page states 3 to 5 working days for EFT-type refunds and 5 to 7 for credit cards once processed [S44, snippet]. Temu customers report refunds denied on courier "attempted delivery" status [S27].
8. **Support deflection.** The most cited Takealot complaint theme is no reachable human [S13]. Same caveat on self-selection.
9. **Forced accounts and slow checkout.** Baymard: 18 percent of abandoners said the site wanted an account; 17 percent said checkout was too long; 62 percent of sites fail to make guest checkout the most prominent option (2026 summary of Baymard) [S1][S3]. Average US checkout shows 23.48 form elements versus roughly 12 ideal [S2].
10. **Sellers with unclear identity.** BEUC says Temu has weak seller traceability [S19]. Takealot's marketplace mixes retailer and third-party offers on one page (K). CGSO notes marketplace operators are expected to admit only legitimate, compliant sellers [S28]. This is the opening for Dot.Emall, because InfoDot business verification can put a real, checked business identity beside every offer.

**South African specifics worth designing for**
- Payments: debit card is the largest online method (58 percent of online transactions per one gateway vendor, unverified) [S26]. BNPL doubled from 3 to 6 percent of PayFast volume 2024 to 2026 with a R1,568 average basket [S26]. Instant EFT (Ozow) is common, and its refunds take 3 to 5 working days [S44]. Cash on delivery exists but Takealot restricts it to selected items and areas [S40]. A share for COD, Ozow and PayFast as checkout methods: GAP.
- Delivery: 97 percent of SA shoppers say delivery options affect where they shop (DHL survey, relayed by a vendor article) [S25]. Pargo pickup points reach just under 4,000 locations and about 87 percent of postal codes (vendor-sourced) [S25]. The Courier Guy admits load shedding, fuel and security disrupt deliveries (self-description) [S39]. Independent complaint data for The Courier Guy and Aramex: GAP.
- Load shedding: affects courier sorters and last mile [S39]; for a web app the practical risk is dropped mobile connections mid-payment, so checkout must be idempotent and recoverable.
- Regulators: the NCC is investigating Temu and Shein under the CPA; no preliminary findings reported [S27].

## 3. The ten most repeated seller frustrations

1. **Stacked fees.** Amazon: referral about 15 percent, FBA 20 to 35 percent, ads up to 15 percent, giving a 50 percent average take in 2022 [S16]. Takealot sellers describe monthly fee plus success fee plus fulfilment plus storage, forcing price rises (anecdote) [S14]; Takealot raised fulfilment fees again from 1 April 2026 [S15]. eBay raised final value fees up to 0.35 points in 2025 [S36].
2. **Opaque ranking.** Takealot's Buy Box rule and its promotion approvals favoured Takealot Retail in the Commission's findings [S12]. Etsy ties visibility to Star Seller metrics sellers find punitive [S29].
3. **Account suspensions.** Amazon sellers report algorithmic suspensions with little explanation, "related account" flags and denied appeals. This is nearly all from appeal-service consultants with commercial interests; no Amazon data on volumes (GAP) [S45].
4. **Return abuse and negative balances.** A Takealot seller calls returns the "most devastating" part, with weeks to process and negative balances (single TikTok account, comments agreeing; anecdote) [S14]. The Commission's 60-day deemed-resolved remedy shows the disputes were real [S12].
5. **Payout delays and holds.** eBay new sellers report 14 to 30 day holds (forum anecdote); eBay says funds release within 72 hours of confirmed delivery in 2025 [S42].
6. **Forced discounts, price parity and loss of price control.** Takealot's narrow price parity clause blocked cheaper pricing on sellers' own sites [S12]. Temu fully managed sets price [S43].
7. **Ad costs that are no longer optional.** Marketplace Pulse: ads became "unavoidable" [S16]. Etsy Offsite Ads are mandatory for shops above USD 10,000 [S29].
8. **Weak analytics.** I found no good evidence in this run (GAP). Hypothesis (K): small sellers want to know why a listing is not selling, not a dashboard of totals.
9. **Listing quality rules and gating.** Takealot gated brands unilaterally [S12]; Temu fined suppliers for packaging defects and listing mismatches [S43].
10. **Platform competing with and learning from sellers.** The Commission found Takealot buyers used seller data to inform own-brand decisions and that disputes sat unresolved so sellers carried the cost [S12].

## 4. Search and discovery at mid-size scale

**What best practice is** (Baymard-style, see S4 for headline statistics; detailed guidelines are paywalled, so GAP on specifics like no-results copy):
- Facets generated from structured attributes per category (size, colour, brand, price, delivery speed, seller location), with counts and removable chips.
- Typo tolerance and "did you mean"; synonym handling including local vocabulary (K): cellphone and mobile, bakkie, geyser, braai, "plug" and "adapter".
- Category-aware search: a query like "samsung" returns phones first if that is the dominant category, and offers category chips.
- Autocomplete from three real sources only: product titles, categories and brands, and past queries that returned results.
- A real "no results" page: show the corrected query, drop one filter at a time with a note, show nearest categories, and offer "alert me when this arrives".

**What PostgreSQL gives without Elasticsearch**
- `tsvector` with a generated column and a GIN index, queried with `websearch_to_tsquery`; weight title above brand above description (K, standard Postgres).
- `pg_trgm` for typos and partial words: `similarity`, `word_similarity`, `%` operators, GIN or GiST trigram indexes that also speed `ILIKE`. Default thresholds 0.3, 0.6 and 0.5 [S30]. It is a "trusted" extension, so a non-superuser with CREATE on the database can install it, which matters on shared hosting [S30].
- `unaccent` removes diacritics, useful for names like Möller or Café. Its function is STABLE, so indexing needs an IMMUTABLE wrapper, and the module must be installed [S30]. Check on the actual host that both extensions can be enabled before designing around them (GAP, host-specific).
- Synonyms: keep a `search_synonyms` table and expand the query in application code. Postgres thesaurus dictionaries need files in the server's share directory, which shared hosting will not give you (K).
- Facet counts: normalised `product_attribute_values` table, not JSONB, so the same query works on SQLite (K).

**SQLite parity in tests.** SQLite has no pg_trgm. Put search behind one interface with a Postgres strategy (tsvector plus trigram) and a portable strategy (LIKE plus a small PHP similarity). Run the ranking and typo cases against both, and keep a Postgres-only test group for extension behaviour. The memory notes show other Dot repos already run SQLite and Postgres CI.

**Honest recommendations at small scale**
- Co-purchase: nightly job counts how many distinct orders contain both A and B; show only above a minimum support (for example 3 orders), labelled "Bought together by 5 customers". This is the item-to-item idea Amazon published, whose compute scales with items rather than users [S31].
- Co-view counts from view events, shown only after enough sessions; "Customers also viewed".
- Recently viewed (private, per session or user).
- Same category and brand, labelled as that. Complementary product relations set by sellers or curators, labelled "Goes with".
- Cold start: new catalogue, no orders. Show "New arrivals" ordered by date and "From verified sellers near you". No "Trending", "Popular" or "Best seller" label unless a number exists and is shown. Never seed fake popularity.

## 5. Checkout, honest pricing, reviews, law

**What demonstrably reduces abandonment.** Baymard reported average abandonment of 70.22 percent across 50 studies; of those who abandoned, 40 percent blamed extra costs, 20 percent slow delivery, 19 percent distrust of card handling, 18 percent forced account, 17 percent long checkout, 17 percent errors, 13 percent returns policy, 12 percent unseen total, 10 percent card declined, 9 percent too few payment methods [S1]. Large-site checkout design alone could lift conversion by about 35 percent in Baymard's modelling [S2]. Concrete patterns:
- Guest checkout as the visible default, labelled "Guest" [S3].
- Total cost, including delivery and VAT to the buyer's address, before the cart.
- Fewer fields; about 12 elements is the benchmark [S2].
- Address entry: autocomplete helps (K), but I found no SA-specific study (GAP). SA addresses include complexes, farms, and informal areas, so always allow manual entry and offer Pargo pickup.
- Say which payment methods are available and what happens next (EFT redirect, card 3D Secure).
- Error recovery: keep the cart, keep typed fields, make payment webhooks idempotent.

**South African law (read as engineering inputs, not legal advice; get an attorney to confirm)**
- ECTA s44: 7 days to cancel without reason or penalty, starting at delivery for goods and at agreement for services; consumer pays only direct return cost; refund within 30 days [S8]. The CPA direct-marketing cooling-off does not apply where s44 does [S28 secondary, mjkinc]. ECTA s42(2) excludes categories (for example personalised goods, perishables, date-specific bookings), but I could not retrieve the full list (GAP) [S46]. Omitting required disclosures can give 14 days to cancel under s43(3) (secondary source) [S46].
- CPA s20: returns for goods the consumer could not inspect and which do not conform, within 10 business days, at supplier's expense; no return if altered or installed [S9]. s56: within six months, repair, replace or refund for goods failing quality standards, at supplier's risk and expense [S10].
- CPA s23: a displayed price binds the supplier except for an inadvertent, obvious error; catalogue specials must show a date period (secondary summaries; primary text not retrieved) [S11]. No explicit "was price" rule found (GAP). CPA s41 (false or misleading representation) is the general hook (K, not fetched).
- POPIA s69: electronic direct marketing needs consent, except an existing-customer soft opt-in where an easy opt-out was offered at collection and in each message; every message must identify the sender [S32]. Opt-out is not consent for non-customers [S32].
- Design inference: show the ECTA window on the order page, make returns self-service, and keep marketing opt-in separate from order emails.

**Honest pricing rules to build in.** (1) A "was" price appears only if that price was actually charged in this marketplace. (2) The reference is the lowest price in the previous 30 days, matching the EU rule [S21], and show the date range. (3) Never show a percent off against a seller-supplied RRP without labelling it "Seller's recommended price". (4) Store price history append-only so it can be audited. (5) No countdowns unless an end timestamp is enforced server-side.

**"Verified purchase" practice.** Label only when the review is tied to a delivered order line. Show the order-to-review gap. Give sellers a public reply, not the power to delete. Publish all reviews that pass spam checks, including negative. Disclose how reviews are checked (the EU requires this [S23]). Defences: one review per delivered order line, rate limits, text similarity and burst detection per product, flag reviews from accounts sharing address, device or payment fingerprint with the seller, and a moderator audit log. Never pay or reward reviews, which the FTC rule bans when conditioned on sentiment [S17].

## 6. Multi-seller order mechanics

**Presentation.** One order, one payment, N shipments. Show "Parcel 1 of 2: from Seller A, arrives Tue 12 Nov" with each parcel's own delivery line and cost, a combined total, and per-seller free-delivery progress ("Add R84 from this seller for free delivery"). Returns and refunds attach to a shipment line, not the whole order. (Takealot's split-shipment presentation: GAP.)

**Paystack, what is actually available.** From the docs [S5][S6]:
- Subaccounts per seller. A transaction split can be percentage (whole numbers only, total at most 100) or flat (fixed amounts in the currency subunit, total cannot exceed the amount). Dynamic splits can be created per transaction by passing a `split` object, and the `charge.success` webhook returns the formula and resulting amounts.
- Bearer of Paystack's fees: main account (default), all, all-proportional, account only, or one named subaccount.
- A split's type cannot be changed after creation.
- Not on the page: maximum subaccounts per split, supported currencies for splits, and minimums. ZAR is listed as supported for South Africa by an SDK page [S7 vendor]; a support article says flat splits have a minimum transaction amount [S6]. Verify all three with Paystack.
- Each subaccount settles on its own schedule (third-party guide, vendor) [S7]. A deactivated subaccount's pending payout is held until re-enabled [S6]. That is a side effect, not a designed escrow.
- Refunds on split transactions: GAP. I found nothing saying whether a refund reverses the subaccount share, the main share or both.
- Escrow or delayed release: not documented anywhere I found. Do not assume it.

**Recommended payout design.** Charge the buyer into the platform's own Paystack balance (no split), keep an internal double-entry ledger (buyer paid, platform commission, seller owed), and release seller payouts after delivery confirmation plus the ECTA 7-day window, using Paystack transfers or a batched EFT. Questions to resolve with Paystack and an attorney before building: whether ZAR transfers to bank accounts are enabled on the account (K: unverified), and whether holding sellers' funds makes the platform a regulated payment intermediary (NPS Act or FICA exposure; not researched, GAP). A split-at-charge design is simpler and avoids holding funds, but loses the ability to claw back from sellers after a refund, so decide by risk appetite.

**Commissions.** Keep commission a line in the ledger per shipment, snapshotted at order time so rate changes never rewrite history. Compare: Takealot about 4 to 18 percent plus R400/month [S14], Walmart referral only [S35], Etsy 6.5 percent plus processing [S29], Bob Shop commission only [S38]. A seller-friendly angle: no monthly fee, a transparent per-category rate, and the fee shown on the listing form before publish.

## 7. Fifteen buildable differentiators

Constraints: shared hosting, no always-on workers, database queue drained by cron, Postgres in production, SQLite in tests.

1. **Total-price-first display.** Why it beats the status quo: 40 percent of abandoners cite extra costs and 12 percent cannot see total up front [S1]. Data: shipping rate tables by seller and postal code, VAT flag, buyer's saved postcode. Risk: rate accuracy per courier; show "estimated" with the source of the rate.
2. **Honest price history.** Why: Which? findings on fake deals [S20]; EU 30-day rule [S21]. Data: append-only price change table. Risk: sparse history for new products, so show nothing until at least 14 days exist rather than inventing a baseline.
3. **"Why am I seeing this" on every recommendation.** Why: BEUC calls out lack of transparency about recommendations [S19]. Data: recommendation source enum (co-purchase N, same brand, recently viewed, seller-set). Risk: small N looks weak; show it anyway, honesty is the point.
4. **Review authenticity ladder.** Rungs: verified delivered order, photo, seller response, "never incentivised" attestation. Why: fake review evidence [S17][S41]. Data: order-line to review link, image upload, seller reply. Risk: low review volume early; do not hide unverified, rank verified first.
5. **Seller trust ladder from real data.** Rungs: InfoDot business verification, fulfilment rate, median dispatch time, response time, dispute rate. Why: counterfeit and identity gaps [S19][S28]. Data: InfoDot verification API, shipment events, message timestamps. Risk: metrics punish small sellers; show a minimum-sample note and never rank by it without disclosing the formula.
6. **Guest checkout with magic-link order tracking.** Why: 18 percent leave over forced accounts [S1]. Data: email, signed token, optional account claim after purchase. Risk: link leakage; short-lived tokens, re-verify before address change or refund.
7. **Local discovery ("sellers near you").** Why: faster delivery, Pargo and local couriers, township surcharge problem [S25]. Data: seller dispatch postcode, courier zones. Risk: privacy of seller addresses; show suburb only.
8. **Catalogue-constrained shopping assistant.** Answers only from structured attributes, specs and policy text, citing the field it used; says "not in the catalogue" otherwise. Why: avoids invented product claims. Data: attribute tables, FAQ. Risk: cost and abuse on shared hosting; run on demand, rate-limit, log, and keep a fallback to plain search.
9. **Saved searches and back-in-stock or price-drop alerts.** Why: search "no results" becomes a retention loop. Data: saved filter JSON, stock and price events. Risk: POPIA consent for marketing; make alerts transactional and opt-in per alert, with easy unsubscribe [S32]. Cron cost: batch per hour.
10. **Returns made simple.** Self-service request, auto-approve within the CPA or ECTA window for eligible reasons, prepaid label where the seller allows, status visible both sides, deadline timers for sellers. Why: refunds are the top Takealot complaint [S13]; the Commission forced a 60-day clock [S12]. Data: return reasons, shipment events. Risk: abuse; track return rate per buyer and per product, never silent.
11. **One-tap reorder.** Why: consumables, repeat purchases. Data: past order lines, current price and stock. Risk: price drift; show changed price before adding.
12. **Collections curated from real data.** Examples: "most reordered this month", "highest verified rating in Kitchen", "new from verified sellers". Why: honest alternative to ads. Data: order and review aggregates with minimum thresholds. Risk: thin early data; hide collections below threshold.
13. **Delivery promise you can audit.** Show promised and actual delivery stats per seller ("delivered within promise 91 percent, 148 orders"). Why: Takealot date-change complaints [S13]. Data: shipped and delivered events. Risk: courier data quality; allow seller to flag courier fault.
14. **Dispute timeline with clocks.** Both parties see deadlines; auto-resolve in the buyer's favour for undisputed return claims after a stated period. Why: Commission remedy shows the need [S12]. Data: dispute events. Risk: abuse; require evidence upload and cap auto-resolution amounts.
15. **Seller "why isn't it selling" diagnostics.** Per listing: impressions, clicks, add-to-cart, abandonment, and rule-based hints (price vs category median, missing attributes, poor photos). Why: addresses weak analytics (GAP evidence, hypothesis). Data: view and cart events, category stats. Risk: event volume on shared hosting; aggregate nightly, store daily counts, not raw rows beyond 30 days.

## 8. Shared-hosting and architecture implications, and risks

**Architecture**
- **Search:** Postgres FTS plus pg_trgm, synonyms table, indexes created in migrations. Cache autocomplete results for popular prefixes for 5 minutes. Check extension availability on the host first.
- **Queues:** database queue, one cron entry per minute running the worker with a stop-when-empty flag and a time limit. Jobs must be idempotent, small, and safe to retry. Use `flock` or the framework's overlap guard so overlapping cron runs cannot double-process. The shared crontab was wiped by concurrent deploys in the past, and flock was merged into all repos for this reason (internal memory note).
- **Payments:** webhook handler must verify the Paystack signature, be idempotent on reference, and reconcile with a scheduled job that polls any "pending" payment older than 10 minutes. Never trust the browser redirect alone.
- **Images:** resize on upload (thumb, card, large) to WebP with a JPEG fallback, store with content hashes, set long cache headers, cap upload size, strip EXIF location. Queue variant generation if the host's PHP time limit is tight.
- **Caching:** file or database cache is enough; cache category pages and facet counts for minutes, invalidate on product change. No Redis assumed.
- **Cron overlap:** daily jobs (co-purchase aggregation, price history rollups, seller metrics) run in chunks with a lock and a checkpoint.
- **Docroot:** serve from `public`, deny file execution in uploads (internal memory notes the docroot lessons from Dopemine and others).

**Risks and design answers**

| Risk | Design answer |
|---|---|
| Payment fraud, stolen cards | 3D Secure via Paystack, velocity limits per account and device, manual review queue above a value threshold, delay payout until delivery |
| Chargebacks | Keep delivery proof (courier event, signature, photo if available), evidence pack export per order, reserve a holdback in the seller ledger |
| Counterfeit goods | Brand gating by evidence from the rights holder, seller attestation, report-listing button with a response SLA, removal and repeat-offender policy, restrict luxury and cosmetics categories until verification is in place |
| Prohibited and regulated items | Category allow list, keyword and image checks at listing, no alcohol, tobacco, firearms, medicines, or adult goods at launch; check legal requirements before widening |
| Minors | Terms require 18 plus or parental consent; no alcohol or adult categories; age attestation for any age-restricted item (K: legal minimum for contracts is a legal question, GAP) |
| POPIA | Minimise data collected, separate marketing consent from order emails [S32], retention schedule, data export and deletion tools, processor agreements with Paystack and couriers, breach plan, watch cross-border transfers if using external address APIs |
| Seller identity abuse | InfoDot verification gate, bank account name match, first-month payout holds, display legal trading name on every offer |
| Review manipulation | Section 5 defences |
| Disputes and unreachable support | Ticketing with SLA clocks visible to buyer and seller, human escalation path, public stats |
| Load-shedding and mobile data interruptions | Resumable checkout, idempotent payments, order status via magic link |
| Regulated money handling | See section 6: confirm with Paystack and an attorney before holding third-party funds |
